At the bottom of a tree

What is soil?

The soft, dark stuff under the grass: tiny bits of rock, rotted leaves, air, water, and more living things than you could ever count.

What is soil made of?

About half of soil is tiny bits of broken rock. A little is rotted leaves and plants. The rest is gaps, filled with air and water. Roots and creatures need both.

Grown-up words: mineral particles is the grown-up word for = ‘tiny bits of broken rock’ organic matter is the grown-up word for = ‘rotted leaves and plants’ pore space is the grown-up word for = ‘gaps’

Where does soil come from?

Rain, ice, plant roots and lichens slowly crumble rock into tiny bits. Dead plants and animals rot into it. Making soil takes a very, very long time, so it’s precious.

Grown-up words: weathering is the grown-up word for = ‘crumble rock into tiny bits’

What are sand, silt and clay?

They’re bits of rock of different sizes. Sand is the biggest: it feels gritty, and water runs straight through it. Clay is the tiniest: it’s sticky, holds on to water and food, and squashes easily. Silt is in between. Most soil is a mix. A mix with a good balance of all three is called loam.

Why do trees need soil?

Soil holds a tree up, and gives its roots water, food and air. Thick roots near the trunk hold it steady like tent pegs: see what stops a tree blowing over.

Grown-up words: nutrients is the grown-up word for = ‘food’

Is soil alive?

Very! Soil is full of worms, beetles, woodlice, fungi, and living things too tiny to see. A handful of good soil holds millions of them. Some are hiding on this page: see if you can find them on the way down.

Grown-up words: bacteria and other microbes is the grown-up word for = ‘living things too tiny to see’

On the ground

What happens to fallen leaves?

They get eaten! An army of creatures and fungi turns them back into soil food, so the tree can use it all over again.

What is leaf litter?

The layer of fallen leaves, twigs and bark on top of the soil is called leaf litter. In a wood, it’s like a blanket that never gets tidied away.

Grown-up words: leaf litter, the O horizon is the grown-up word for = ‘leaf litter’

Who eats the leaves first?

Shredders: woodlice, millipedes, slugs and earthworms. They nibble big leaves into small bits, and their poo is full of tiny pieces that are easier for others to eat.

Grown-up words: detritivores is the grown-up word for = ‘Shredders’

What do earthworms do?

Earthworms pull leaves down into their burrows and eat them with soil. Their poo, called worm casts, is rich, crumbly soil. Their tunnels let air and rain in. Charles Darwin was so amazed by worms that he wrote a whole book about them.

Grown-up words: casts is the grown-up word for = ‘worm casts’

Who finishes the job?

Springtails and mites, smaller than a grain of rice, nibble the tiniest bits. Then fungi and bacteria break down what’s left, until it’s dark, crumbly soil food.

Grown-up words: Collembola is the grown-up word for = ‘Springtails’ soil mites is the grown-up word for = ‘mites’ humus is the grown-up word for = ‘dark, crumbly soil food’

Should we sweep up leaves under trees?

On paths, yes: wet leaves are slippery. But on soil and flower beds under a tree, leaving them feeds the soil and its creatures. In a wood, nobody sweeps up, and the trees do fine. You can also pile leaves up to turn into crumbly leaf compost.

Grown-up words: leaf mould is the grown-up word for = ‘crumbly leaf compost’

A blanket for roots

Why put mulch round a tree?

A layer of wood chip or leaves copies the forest floor. It keeps soil damp, soft and full of life.

What is mulch?

Mulch is a layer of something spread on top of soil. For trees, the best is chunky wood chip, like the chip tree surgeons make, or leaves.

Grown-up words: coarse arborist wood chip is the grown-up word for = ‘chunky wood chip’

What does mulch do?

It stops the soil drying out, keeps weeds down, and stops feet squashing the soil. As it slowly rots, it feeds the creatures, and they feed the soil. Roots grow better under it.

Grown-up words: reducing evaporation is the grown-up word for = ‘drying out’

How do you put mulch down?

Spread it in a wide circle, about 5 to 10 centimetres deep, like a doughnut. Leave a gap round the trunk. Piled up against the trunk like a volcano, it keeps the bark wet, and the bark can rot.

Grown-up words: mulch depth is the grown-up word for = ‘about 5 to 10 centimetres deep’ mulch ring is the grown-up word for = ‘doughnut’ volcano mulching is the grown-up word for = ‘volcano’

Does mulch protect the trunk?

Yes. With a ring of mulch, mowers and strimmers stay away from the trunk. Their bumps and cuts on the bark are a way in for germs: see how fungi get into a tree.

Grown-up words: mechanical injury is the grown-up word for = ‘bumps and cuts on the bark’

Is fresh wood chip all right?

Yes, spread on top of the soil. It only borrows a little plant food right where it touches the surface, not down where the roots are. Don’t dig it in, though.

Grown-up words: nitrogen is the grown-up word for = ‘plant food’

In the topsoil

What food is in soil?

Minerals, a bit like vitamins for trees. They dissolve in the water in the soil, like sugar in tea, and roots drink them up.

What do trees need from soil?

Trees make their own sugar from sunshine, but they need minerals from the soil: nitrogen for leaves, phosphorus for energy, potassium for opening and shutting leaf mouths, and tiny bits of others, like iron. See what minerals a tree needs.

Grown-up words: mineral nutrients is the grown-up word for = ‘minerals’ N is the grown-up word for = ‘nitrogen’ P is the grown-up word for = ‘phosphorus’ K is the grown-up word for = ‘potassium’

Where do the minerals come from?

Most come back from rotting leaves, poo and dead things: that’s recycling. Some come slowly out of the bits of rock. And some tiny living things grab nitrogen out of the air.

Grown-up words: nutrient cycling is the grown-up word for = ‘recycling’ nitrogen fixation is the grown-up word for = ‘tiny living things grab nitrogen out of the air’

How do roots get the minerals?

The minerals mix into the water and vanish, like sugar in tea, and the thinnest roots soak them up. Fungi wrapped round the roots help, reaching much further: see why tree roots need fungi.

Grown-up words: dissolve is the grown-up word for = ‘mix into the water and vanish’

Why is topsoil darker?

Topsoil has the most rotted leaves and plants in it, and that makes it dark. It’s where most creatures live, and where most of a tree’s drinking roots are.

Grown-up words: organic matter is the grown-up word for = ‘rotted leaves and plants’

Can a tree run short of food?

In a wood, fallen leaves keep the food coming back. In streets, parks and gardens, leaves are often swept away and the soil gets squashed, so less comes back. Mulch helps put that right. Pouring on plant food usually isn’t the answer: see do trees need plant food?

Under our feet

Why is squashed soil bad for trees?

Soil needs its gaps. Squash them shut and roots can’t breathe, drink or grow.

What are the gaps for?

Roots need air to breathe, just like you. The gaps hold the air, let rain soak in, and give roots and creatures room to move.

What squashes soil?

Feet, bikes, cars and big diggers. Feet mostly squash the top few centimetres. Heavy machines squash much deeper. Wet soil squashes most easily of all.

Grown-up words: surface compaction is the grown-up word for = ‘top few centimetres’

What happens to the tree?

Its roots can’t push through, rain runs off instead of soaking in, and the roots run short of air. The tree slowly gets weaker, and it can take years to show.

How do we protect a tree’s soil?

During building work, a fence goes round a big area of soil round the tree, called the root protection area. Nobody drives or stores anything in there. Paths, boards and mulch help too.

Grown-up words: RPA, from BS 5837 is the grown-up word for = ‘root protection area’

Can squashed soil be mended?

Slowly. Worms, roots and frost open up the gaps again over the years, and mulch feeds the worms. Tree surgeons can also loosen the soil round roots with a tool that blasts air.

Grown-up words: air spade is the grown-up word for = ‘a tool that blasts air’

In the subsoil

What’s deeper down?

Paler, harder soil, with less air, fewer creatures and fewer roots.

What is subsoil?

Under the dark topsoil is paler soil. It has much less rotted stuff in it, and less air, so fewer creatures and roots live there.

Grown-up words: subsoil is the grown-up word for = ‘paler soil’

How deep do tree roots go?

Not as deep as you’d think! Most stay in the top part of the soil, where there’s air, but they spread out wide, often further than the branches. See how deep tree roots go.

Who digs down here?

Some earthworms are deep diggers: their burrows go more than a metre down. Moles dig tunnels, hunting for them.

Grown-up words: anecic earthworms is the grown-up word for = ‘deep diggers’

Why are soils different colours?

Dark brown or black soil has lots of rotted stuff in it. Red, orange or yellow soil has rusty iron in it. Grey or blue-grey soil is often soaked, with no air.

Grown-up words: iron oxides is the grown-up word for = ‘rusty iron’ gleyed soil is the grown-up word for = ‘soaked, with no air’

At the very bottom

What’s at the very bottom?

Solid rock. Very slowly, it crumbles and becomes new soil.

What is bedrock?

Under all the soil is solid rock, called bedrock. Sometimes it’s just under the surface; sometimes it’s many metres down.

How does rock turn into soil?

Water seeps into cracks and freezes. Ice takes up more room than water, so it pushes the cracks wider. Roots grow into cracks and push too. Bit by bit, rock breaks into stones, then sand, then silt. Rainwater slowly changes some of the tiniest bits into sticky clay. That’s how soil is made, and it takes thousands of years.

Grown-up words: soil formation is the grown-up word for = ‘how soil is made’

Why should we look after soil?

Because it takes so long to make, and almost all our food grows in it. Look after the soil, and it looks after the trees.
