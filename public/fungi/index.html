How do fungi work?

Most fungi on trees are opportunists: they wait for a chance. 48 questions about what that chance is, and what fungi do when they get it. 5 diagrams to play with.

In the answers, hover over or tap any underlined words to see their grown-up word.

Meet the fungi

What is a fungus?

A living thing that isn’t a plant or an animal. It can’t make its own food, so it eats other things, and most of it is hidden.

Is a fungus a plant?

No. A plant makes its own food from sunshine. A fungus can’t, so it feeds on other things, a bit like we do. Fungi are a group of living things all of their own.

Grown-up words: heterotroph is the grown-up word for = ‘feeds on other things’ kingdom Fungi is the grown-up word for = ‘a group of living things all of their own’

Where is the rest of the fungus?

Most of a fungus is a web of tiny threads, each thinner than a hair. The threads grow through wood or soil, eating as they go. The mushroom or bracket you see is only the fungus’s fruit.

Grown-up words: mycelium is the grown-up word for = ‘a web of tiny threads’ hypha (plural: hyphae) is the grown-up word for = ‘thinner than a hair’ fruiting body is the grown-up word for = ‘the fungus’s fruit’

How does a fungus eat?

It has no mouth. The threads leak out juices that break food into tiny pieces, then soak the pieces up. It eats from the outside in.

Grown-up words: enzymes is the grown-up word for = ‘leak out juices that break food into tiny pieces’ absorption is the grown-up word for = ‘soak the pieces up’

What are spores?

A fungus’s fruit lets go of tiny specks, like seeds, far too small to see. They drift on the wind, so spores land on trees all the time. Most land somewhere they can’t grow, and nothing happens.

Grown-up words: spores is the grown-up word for = ‘tiny specks, like seeds’

Inside the wood

What is wood made of?

Millions of tiny tubes, with walls made of strong strings and soaked in woody glue.

What are the tubes made of?

Each tube’s wall is strong strings wound round and round in layers, soaked in a tough, woody glue. More glue sits between the tubes, holding them together.

Grown-up words: cellulose is the grown-up word for = ‘strong strings’ lignin is the grown-up word for = ‘a tough, woody glue’ middle lamella is the grown-up word for = ‘between the tubes’

Is paper made of wood?

Yes! Paper is the wood strings, pulled apart, with most of the glue taken out, then pressed flat. Tear some paper and look closely at the edge: the fuzzy bits are the strings. Newspaper keeps more of the glue in, which is why old newspaper goes yellow in the sun.

Grown-up words: cellulose fibres is the grown-up word for = ‘wood strings’

Why is wood so hard to eat?

The strings are wrapped up in glue, and the glue is very hard to break down. Hardly anything can eat it. People can’t even eat the strings: that’s the fibre in our food. Some fungi can eat both, which makes them the forest’s big recyclers.

Grown-up words: dietary fibre is the grown-up word for = ‘fibre in our food’ decomposers is the grown-up word for = ‘big recyclers’

Waiting for a chance

Why don’t fungi eat every tree?

Healthy, working wood is wet and fights back. Most fungi wait for a chance: wood that has stopped working.

Why can’t fungi eat healthy wood?

Wood that’s carrying water is full of water, so there’s hardly any air inside, and most fungi need air. Its living cells fight back with chemicals, too.

Grown-up words: high moisture content is the grown-up word for = ‘full of water’ low oxygen is the grown-up word for = ‘hardly any air inside’ parenchyma is the grown-up word for = ‘living cells’

What is an opportunist?

Someone who waits for a chance, then grabs it. Most wood-eating fungi are like that. They can’t break into healthy wood, so they wait for wood that has stopped working.

Grown-up words: dysfunctional wood is the grown-up word for = ‘stopped working’

What happens when you cut off a branch?

Every leaf sits at the top of lots of drinking straws running down through the wood. The leaves pull water up those straws. Cut the branch off and nothing pulls any more. The straws in the stub fill with air and dry out, and the living cells nearby die. That wood has stopped working.

Grown-up words: xylem vessels is the grown-up word for = ‘lots of drinking straws’ embolism is the grown-up word for = ‘fill with air’ dysfunction is the grown-up word for = ‘stopped working’

Why do fungi move into the dry wood?

Dry, airy wood with nothing left to fight back is just what a fungus wants. Spores land on the cut, or were already hiding inside, and the fungus moves in. The tree builds walls to keep it in that one part: see how a tree deals with a wound.

Grown-up words: latent colonisation is the grown-up word for = ‘were already hiding inside’ compartmentalisation (CODIT) is the grown-up word for = ‘walls’

Why do big cuts matter?

The bigger the cut, the more straws stop working, and the more dry wood there is for fungi. Taking lots of leaves off a tree at once, like chopping its top off, does the same all over the tree. That’s why tree surgeons try to make small cuts, and why when you prune matters too.

Grown-up words: topping is the grown-up word for = ‘chopping its top off’ minimising wound size is the grown-up word for = ‘make small cuts’

Does cutting roots do the same?

Yes. Roots are the bottom end of the straws. Cut a big root and the wood it was feeding stops working too, and fungi can get in from below.

Grown-up words: dysfunction is the grown-up word for = ‘stops working too’

Is a snapped branch different from a sawn one?

When a branch snaps, the wood mostly pulls apart between the tubes, along the glue, leaving a jagged, splintery end. A saw slices straight across the tubes and leaves them cut open. On some old trees, tree surgeons copy snapping on purpose with jagged cuts, to make homes for beetles and fungi. Whether a snapped end rots more slowly than a sawn one hasn’t been tested properly yet, but here at The Tree Fella we can’t help noticing much less rot around natural snaps than around pruning cuts.

Grown-up words: intercellular fracture is the grown-up word for = ‘pulls apart between the tubes’ transverse cut is the grown-up word for = ‘slices straight across the tubes’ coronet cuts, natural fracture pruning is the grown-up word for = ‘jagged cuts’

In the middle

What’s in the middle of a tree?

Old wood that has stopped carrying water. Some trees fill it with chemicals that fungi hate. Others don’t.

What is heartwood?

As a tree grows, the old wood in the middle stops carrying water and its living cells die. In many trees it fills up with chemicals that fungi hate and turns darker. That’s heartwood.

Grown-up words: non-conducting wood is the grown-up word for = ‘stops carrying water’ extractives is the grown-up word for = ‘chemicals that fungi hate’ heartwood is the grown-up word for = ‘heartwood’

Do all trees protect their middle?

No. Oak and sweet chestnut fill theirs with bitter chemicals, the same sort that make strong tea taste bitter. Oak also plugs its old straws with bubbles of cell, so water and air can’t get in. Other trees, like beech and birch, don’t make a protected middle. Their middle rots more easily.

Grown-up words: tannins is the grown-up word for = ‘bitter chemicals’ tyloses is the grown-up word for = ‘plugs its old straws with bubbles of cell’ no true heartwood is the grown-up word for = ‘don’t make a protected middle’

What lines the tree’s walls?

When a tree builds a wall round damage, it can line it with a waxy, corky stuff, the same stuff that makes cork waterproof. It’s mostly in the walls and the bark, not the heartwood.

Grown-up words: suberin is the grown-up word for = ‘a waxy, corky stuff’

Which fungi eat heartwood?

Some fungi are heartwood specialists. They can live where there’s hardly any air and lots of tree chemicals. Many are fussy and only live in one kind of tree, like the beefsteak fungus, which lives on oak and sweet chestnut.

Grown-up words: heart rot fungi is the grown-up word for = ‘heartwood specialists’ low oxygen, high carbon dioxide, phenolic compounds is the grown-up word for = ‘hardly any air and lots of tree chemicals’ Fistulina hepatica is the grown-up word for = ‘beefsteak fungus’

Is a tree with heart rot going to die?

Not usually. The heartwood isn’t carrying water, so the tree can carry on for years and years while the middle slowly hollows out. Chicken of the woods is famous for hollowing out very old oaks. A hollow tree can still be strong: see is a hollow tree always weak?

Grown-up words: hollowing is the grown-up word for = ‘hollows out’ Laetiporus sulphureus is the grown-up word for = ‘Chicken of the woods’

Getting in

How do fungi get into a tree?

Four ways. Three of them wait for a chance. Only a few fungi can break in on their own.

Which fungi wait at a wound?

When bark is torn off or a branch is cut, the sapwood underneath dries out in the air. Lots of different fungi race to grab it, like dryad’s saddle, a big scaly bracket shaped like a saddle.

Grown-up words: exposed sapwood is the grown-up word for = ‘dries out in the air’ Cerioporus squamosus is the grown-up word for = ‘dryad’s saddle’

Which fungi hide inside?

Some fungi sneak in while the tree is healthy, through tiny breathing holes, then wait inside the wood, doing no harm. If the wood dries out, in a drought, they wake up and spread fast. The birch polypore lives in birch trees like this.

Grown-up words: lenticels and stomata is the grown-up word for = ‘tiny breathing holes’ latent invasion is the grown-up word for = ‘wait inside the wood’ water stress is the grown-up word for = ‘drought’ Fomitopsis betulina is the grown-up word for = ‘birch polypore’

Can a fungus hide in an acorn?

Yes! Scientists have found one of these hiding fungi inside acorns, but only in a few acorns, from oak trees that were already poorly. The young trees that grew from those acorns didn’t have it.

Grown-up words: Biscogniauxia mediterranea is the grown-up word for = ‘one of these hiding fungi’

Which fungi can break in?

A few fungi can attack living, working wood on their own. Honey fungus spreads from old stumps through the soil in black bootlaces and attacks tree roots. Fungi like this are rare: most fungi on trees are opportunists.

Grown-up words: Armillaria is the grown-up word for = ‘Honey fungus’ rhizomorphs is the grown-up word for = ‘black bootlaces’ pathogens is the grown-up word for = ‘rare’

Rot

Does all rot look the same?

No. Different fungi eat different parts of the wood, so the rot they leave looks different.

What is white rot?

Some fungi eat the glue, and often the strings too. What’s left is pale, soft and stringy, like wet paper. Some eat the glue first; others eat everything at once.

Grown-up words: lignin is the grown-up word for = ‘the glue’ selective delignification is the grown-up word for = ‘the glue first’ simultaneous white rot is the grown-up word for = ‘eat everything at once’

What is brown rot?

Other fungi eat the strings and leave the glue. The wood goes brown, shrinks and cracks into little cubes, then crumbles to powder. Instead of juices, these fungi fire tiny chemical sparks at the strings.

Grown-up words: cellulose and hemicellulose is the grown-up word for = ‘the strings’ cubical cracking is the grown-up word for = ‘cracks into little cubes’ free radicals is the grown-up word for = ‘fire tiny chemical sparks’

What is soft rot?

A few fungi tunnel inside the tube walls, leaving chains of tiny holes. The wood goes soft. Brittle cinder does this at the bottom of trees, and looks like a patch of burnt charcoal.

Grown-up words: cavities in the S2 layer is the grown-up word for = ‘tunnel inside the tube walls’ Kretzschmaria deusta is the grown-up word for = ‘Brittle cinder’

Which rot makes a tree break?

Wood with some white rots bends a long way before it breaks. Brown rot, soft rot and the white rots that eat everything at once can make wood snap like a carrot, with little warning. That’s why tree surgeons want to know which fungus is there, not just that there is one.

Grown-up words: ductile fracture is the grown-up word for = ‘bends a long way before it breaks’ brittle fracture is the grown-up word for = ‘snap like a carrot’

Meet a bracket

What is Ganoderma?

A family of big, tough bracket fungi that rot the middle of trees, usually from near the bottom.

What does Ganoderma look like?

Ganoderma is a family of fungi. Its brackets are hard and woody, and stick out from the trunk like shelves, often near the ground. Some add a new layer every year, so they stay on a tree for years. The lacquered bracket looks shiny, as if it’s been varnished.

Grown-up words: genus is the grown-up word for = ‘family of fungi’ perennial fruiting bodies is the grown-up word for = ‘add a new layer every year’ Ganoderma resinaceum is the grown-up word for = ‘lacquered bracket’

Why is it covered in cocoa powder?

That brown dust is its spores. There are so many that they settle all over the top of the bracket, and on the trunk and ground around it.

Grown-up words: basidiospores is the grown-up word for = ‘spores’

Why is one called the artist’s bracket?

The underside of the artist’s bracket is creamy white. Scratch it and the scratch turns dark brown, so people draw pictures on it. A tiny fly lays its eggs in it, which makes little bumps. It doesn’t do this to the southern bracket, so the bumps help tell them apart.

Grown-up words: Ganoderma applanatum is the grown-up word for = ‘the artist’s bracket’ yellow flat-footed fly, Agathomyia wankowiczii is the grown-up word for = ‘fly’ galls is the grown-up word for = ‘little bumps’ Ganoderma australe is the grown-up word for = ‘southern bracket’

What does Ganoderma do to a tree?

It mostly rots the middle with white rot, low down in the trunk and big roots, and some kinds can spread into the working wood too. If enough wood rots, the trunk can break or the tree can blow over. So when tree surgeons see Ganoderma, they check how much good wood is left.

Grown-up words: heart rot is the grown-up word for = ‘rots the middle’ extend into sapwood is the grown-up word for = ‘spread into the working wood’

Making trees ill

Which fungi make trees ill?

Only a few. They attack the living, working parts of a tree, and many arrived here from other countries.

What is ash dieback?

Ash dieback is a fungus that came from eastern Asia. In summer it grows tiny white cups on last year’s fallen leaf stalks on the ground. Their spores blow onto ash leaves, and the fungus grows from the leaf into the twig and the wood. Twigs and branches die back from the tips. A few ash trees can fight it off.

Grown-up words: Hymenoscyphus fraxineus is the grown-up word for = ‘Ash dieback’ native range is the grown-up word for = ‘eastern Asia’ apothecia is the grown-up word for = ‘tiny white cups’ leaf rachises is the grown-up word for = ‘last year’s fallen leaf stalks’ dieback is the grown-up word for = ‘die back from the tips’ tolerance is the grown-up word for = ‘fight it off’

What is Dutch elm disease?

Dutch elm disease is a fungus carried by little beetles that tunnel under elm bark. It grows inside the water straws. The tree tries to stop it by plugging its own straws, but that stops the water too, so the leaves wilt and go yellow. Peel back the bark and there are brown streaks in the wood. It has killed millions of elm trees in Britain.

Grown-up words: Ophiostoma novo-ulmi is the grown-up word for = ‘Dutch elm disease’ elm bark beetles is the grown-up word for = ‘little beetles’ tyloses and gums is the grown-up word for = ‘plugging its own straws’ wilting is the grown-up word for = ‘wilt and go yellow’ vascular staining is the grown-up word for = ‘brown streaks’

What is Phytophthora?

Phytophthora looks like a fungus, but it isn’t one. It’s a water mould. Its spores swim through wet soil to the tips of tree roots. It kills the thin roots that drink, and the inner bark at the bottom of the trunk, so the tree can’t get enough water. Sometimes dark, sticky drips ooze out of the bark.

Grown-up words: Phytophthora is the grown-up word for = ‘Phytophthora’ oomycete is the grown-up word for = ‘water mould’ zoospores is the grown-up word for = ‘swim’ collar rot is the grown-up word for = ‘inner bark at the bottom of the trunk’ bleeding canker is the grown-up word for = ‘dark, sticky drips’

How do we stop them spreading?

Tree germs hitch a lift on boots, tools and new plants. Tree surgeons clean their kit and report poorly trees to tree scientists. You can help: clean the mud off your boots before you go to a different wood or park.

Grown-up words: pathways is the grown-up word for = ‘boots, tools and new plants’ biosecurity is the grown-up word for = ‘clean their kit’ Forest Research’s TreeAlert is the grown-up word for = ‘tree scientists’

Fungi and the forest

Why do forests need fungi?

Without them, dead wood would pile up and up, and lots of animals would have nowhere to live.

What if nothing ate dead wood?

Dead trees and branches would hardly rot at all, and the forest floor would fill up with wood. Fungi turn it back into food for the soil, so new trees can grow.

Grown-up words: decomposition is the grown-up word for = ‘hardly rot at all’ nutrient cycling is the grown-up word for = ‘food for the soil’

Are hollow trees useful?

Very! Hollows made by heart rot fungi become homes for bats, owls and beetles. Dead wood is busy: see is dead wood useful?

Are some fungi the tree’s friends?

Yes. Fungi wrapped round tree roots swap water and minerals for sugar: see why tree roots need fungi.

Can I touch or eat fungi on trees?

Never eat wild fungi. Some deadly ones look just like safe ones. Look at them, draw them and take photos instead, and wash your hands if you touch one. If you spot a big bracket at the bottom of a tree near a path or playground, tell a grown-up.
